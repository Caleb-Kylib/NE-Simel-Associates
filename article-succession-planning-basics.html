Succession Planning Basics in Kenya: Protecting Your Family's Future

8 min read July 3, 2026

Few things cause more family conflict than an estate left without a clear plan. In Kenya, disputes over inheritance can drag on for years, straining relationships and depleting the very assets meant to provide for loved ones. Succession planning, done properly and early, is the surest way to protect your family and honour your wishes.

1. The Legal Framework: The Law of Succession Act

Succession in Kenya is primarily governed by the Law of Succession Act (Cap 160). It sets out how a deceased person's property is distributed, whether or not they left a valid will, and establishes the procedures for administering an estate. The Act applies to most Kenyans, with certain exceptions for estates governed by Islamic law.

2. Testate vs. Intestate Succession

The single most important decision is whether to leave a valid will. The difference is significant:

Testate (With a Will) Intestate (No Will)
Who decides distribution You, the testator The Law of Succession Act formula
Executor / Administrator Chosen by you Appointed by the court
Speed & cost Generally faster and cheaper Often slower and more contested
Risk of dispute Reduced with clear wording Higher

3. What Makes a Will Valid in Kenya

For a written will to be valid under the Law of Succession Act, it must generally meet these requirements:

  • Capacity: The maker must be of sound mind and at least 18 years old.
  • Intention: It must be made freely, without coercion or undue influence.
  • Signature: Signed by the testator (or by someone at their direction).
  • Witnesses: Attested by at least two competent witnesses who are not beneficiaries.

4. Dependants Cannot Be Ignored

A common misconception is that a will allows a person to disinherit their dependants entirely. Kenyan courts have made clear that this is not so. Under Section 26 of the Law of Succession Act, a dependant who has been left out or inadequately provided for may apply to the court for reasonable provision.

Testamentary freedom is not absolute. The court may interfere with the terms of a will where reasonable provision has not been made for a dependant of the deceased.

5. The Probate and Administration Process

After death, the estate must be formally administered before assets can be distributed:

Step 1

Apply for Grant

Probate (where there is a will) or letters of administration (where there is none).

Step 2

Gazettement

The application is published to allow objections.

Step 3

Confirmation of Grant

After the statutory period, the grant is confirmed by the court.

Step 4

Distribution

Assets are transferred to beneficiaries as directed.
