Recent Family Law Updates in Kenya: Key Changes and Implications

9 min read June 12, 2026

Family law in Kenya has evolved significantly over the past decade, driven by progressive legislation and a series of landmark court decisions. From how matrimonial property is shared on divorce to the paramount importance of a child's best interests, the legal landscape now places fairness and dignity at its centre. This article highlights the key developments every family should understand.

1. Matrimonial Property: Contribution Matters

The Matrimonial Property Act, 2013 governs how property is divided when a marriage ends. A central question has been whether property should be split equally or according to each spouse's contribution. The Supreme Court settled much of this debate.

Marriage does not automatically entitle a spouse to a 50 per cent share of matrimonial property. Division depends on the proven contribution of each party, which may be monetary or non-monetary.

— Principle from Joseph Ombogi Ogentoto v. Martha Bosibori Ogentoto (Supreme Court, 2023)

Crucially, the courts recognise non-monetary contribution, such as childcare, homemaking, and supporting a spouse's career, as valuable. This protects spouses, often women, who contribute in ways that are not easily measured in money.

2. Division of Property: Key Considerations

Factor How Courts Treat It
Monetary Contribution Direct financial input toward acquisition or improvement
Non-Monetary Contribution Domestic work, childcare, and management of the home
Property Registration Registration in one name does not defeat the other spouse's claim
Pre-Marital Property Generally excluded unless improved during the marriage

3. Child Custody and the Best Interests Principle

The Children Act, 2022 reaffirms that in all matters concerning children, the best interests of the child are of paramount importance, echoing Article 53 of the Constitution. Courts no longer assume that custody automatically belongs to the mother; instead, they assess each case on its facts.

  • Joint parental responsibility: Both parents share duties regardless of marital status.
  • Child's views: A child of sufficient age and maturity may have their opinion considered.
  • Stability and welfare: Courts prioritise the child's emotional, educational and physical wellbeing.
  • Maintenance: Both parents are obligated to provide for the child.

4. Recognition of Different Marriage Types

The Marriage Act, 2014 consolidated Kenya's marriage laws, recognising Christian, civil, customary, Hindu and Islamic marriages. All registered marriages now enjoy equal legal status, and the Act sets clear requirements for validity, registration, and dissolution. Couples in customary or "come-we-stay" arrangements should understand that presumption of marriage claims have become harder to sustain in recent jurisprudence.

5. Divorce and Dispute Resolution

Kenyan courts increasingly encourage alternative dispute resolution in family matters, promoting mediation to reduce the emotional and financial toll of litigation. Where divorce proceeds to court, grounds must still be established according to the type of marriage, and matters of property, custody, and maintenance are resolved with fairness as the guiding principle.
