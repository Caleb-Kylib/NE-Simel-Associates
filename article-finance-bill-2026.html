Finance Bill 2026 Explained: What Every Kenyan Taxpayer Should Know

10 min read June 5, 2026

Each year, Kenya's Finance Bill sets the tone for the country's tax landscape, determining how much households and businesses pay and how the government funds its budget. The Finance Bill 2026 has attracted intense public interest, particularly after recent years in which tax proposals sparked nationwide debate. This guide explains the key proposals in clear terms and what they mean for you.

1. The Role of the Finance Bill in Kenya

The Finance Bill is the legal instrument through which the government proposes changes to tax laws such as the Income Tax Act, the Value Added Tax Act, 2013, and the Excise Duty Act, 2015. Once passed by the National Assembly and assented to by the President, it becomes the Finance Act and takes effect, usually from 1 July.

Importantly, the Bill must go through public participation, a constitutional requirement under Article 118 of the Constitution. This step has become especially significant following recent litigation over how tax laws are enacted.

2. Key Proposed Changes

While the Bill is subject to amendment during debate, the major proposals typically affect the following areas:

  • Income Tax: Adjustments to personal relief, tax bands, and treatment of allowances.
  • Value Added Tax: Movement of certain goods and services between exempt, zero-rated, and standard-rated categories.
  • Excise Duty: Revised rates on items such as fuel, alcohol, and digital services.
  • Digital Economy: Continued refinement of the digital services tax and rules for online businesses.
  • Tax Administration: Enhanced KRA enforcement powers and compliance measures.

4. The Constitutional Dimension: Lessons from the Courts

The process of enacting finance legislation has itself become a legal battleground. In a landmark decision, the Court of Appeal found significant parts of the Finance Act, 2023 unconstitutional, largely on grounds relating to inadequate public participation and procedure.

Where amendments introduced after public participation are substantial and fundamentally alter the Bill, fresh public participation is required for those changes to be valid.

— Principle from Kenyan Finance Act litigation, Court of Appeal (2024)

This jurisprudence, arising from consolidated petitions challenging the Finance Act, 2023, means the government must genuinely consider public views before enacting tax changes. Taxpayers and businesses therefore have a real opportunity to shape the final law by participating in the process.

5. How to Prepare

  • Participate: Submit views to the National Assembly during the public participation window.
  • Model the impact: Assess how proposed changes affect your payroll, pricing and cash flow.
  • Review contracts: Check whether tax clauses in agreements need updating.
  • Stay compliant: Ensure KRA filings and payments are up to date to avoid penalties.
  • Seek advice: Complex changes may require tailored tax planning.
