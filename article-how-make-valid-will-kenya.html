How to Make a Valid Will in Kenya

9 min read February 5, 2026

Creating a valid will in Kenya requires adherence to specific legal formalities under the Law of Succession Act. A properly executed will ensures your estate is distributed according to your wishes and minimizes the risk of legal challenges.

1. Testamentary Capacity

You must have the legal capacity to make a will. This means:

  • Being at least 18 years old (or married, in military service, or with court permission if younger)
  • Being of sound mind at the time of making the will
  • Understanding the nature of the act and its consequences
  • Knowing the extent of your property
  • Understanding who your natural beneficiaries are

2. Essential Requirements for a Valid Will

Under Section 11 of the Law of Succession Act, a will must:

A. Be in Writing

  • Can be handwritten (holographic will) or typed
  • Must be clear and unambiguous
  • Should be dated for clarity

B. Be Signed by the Testator

  • Must be signed by the person making the will (testator)
  • Signature should be at the end of the will
  • If unable to sign, a thumbprint or mark in the presence of witnesses

C. Proper Witnessing

The will must be attested by at least two competent witnesses who:

  • Are present together when the testator signs
  • See the testator sign or acknowledge the signature
  • Sign the will in the presence of the testator
  • Are not beneficiaries or spouses of beneficiaries

3. Common Types of Wills in Kenya

A. Simple Will

Suitable for straightforward estates with clear distribution wishes.

B. Testamentary Trust Will

Creates a trust within the will, useful for minor children or beneficiaries with special needs.

C. Joint Will

Made by two people (usually spouses) that takes effect after both have died.

D. Living Will

Specifies healthcare wishes in case of incapacity (not strictly a succession document).

4. What to Include in Your Will

  • Personal details: Full name, ID number, address
  • Revocation clause: Statement revoking all previous wills
  • Appointment of executor: Person to administer the estate
  • Guardianship provisions: For minor children if applicable
  • Specific bequests: Particular items to specific people
  • Residual estate: Distribution of remaining property
  • Signatures: Testator and witnesses with dates

5. Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Beneficiaries as witnesses: Invalidates their benefit
  • Unclear language: Leads to interpretation disputes
  • Missing revocation clause: Multiple wills cause confusion
  • Failure to update: Life changes (marriage, divorce, births) require updates
  • Improper storage: Lost or damaged wills are ineffective

6. Storing and Updating Your Will

Once executed, your will should be:

  • Stored securely (safe deposit box, with lawyer, or registrar)
  • Updated after major life events
  • Destroyed properly if revoked (tear, burn, or obliterate)
  • Registered with the Registrar of Documents (optional but recommended)
