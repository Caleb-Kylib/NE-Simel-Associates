Know Your Rights: A Guide to Constitutional Rights and Freedoms in Kenya

7 min read July 10, 2026

The Constitution of Kenya, 2010 is often described as one of the most progressive in the world. At its heart lies the Bill of Rights, Chapter Four, which guarantees a wide range of fundamental rights and freedoms to every person. Understanding these rights, and knowing how to enforce them, is the first step to holding power to account and protecting your dignity.

1. The Bill of Rights: The Foundation

Article 19 of the Constitution declares that the Bill of Rights is an integral part of Kenya's democratic state and the framework for social, economic and cultural policies. Importantly, these rights belong to each individual and are not granted by the state, they are recognised and protected by it.

2. Key Rights and Freedoms

Among the most frequently invoked rights are:

Right / Freedom Article What It Protects
Equality & Freedom from Discrimination Article 27 Equal treatment regardless of sex, race, or other status
Human Dignity Article 28 The inherent worth of every person
Privacy Article 31 Personal information, communications and property
Freedom of Expression Article 33 Speech, opinion and the media (with limits)
Fair Hearing Article 50 Access to justice and due process
Rights of Arrested Persons Article 49 Safeguards during arrest and detention

3. Rights Are Not Always Absolute

Most rights may be limited, but only within strict boundaries. Article 24 provides that a right may be limited only by law, and only to the extent that the limitation is reasonable and justifiable in an open and democratic society. Certain rights, however, such as freedom from torture and slavery, can never be limited.

Any limitation of a fundamental right must be lawful, necessary, and proportionate. The burden of justifying a limitation rests on the party seeking to impose it.

— Principle under Article 24, Constitution of Kenya, 2010

Kenyan courts have given real force to these protections. In the well-known case concerning the criminal offence of defamation, Jacqueline Okuta & Another v. Attorney General & Others [2017] eKLR, the High Court struck down criminal defamation as an unjustifiable limitation on freedom of expression, showing how the courts guard constitutional freedoms.

4. Enforcing Your Rights

Article 22 gives every person the right to go to court to claim that a right in the Bill of Rights has been denied, violated, infringed or threatened. The Constitution deliberately relaxed the rules on who may sue, so that:

  • You can act in your own interest.
  • Someone else can act on behalf of a person who cannot act for themselves.
  • A group or association can act in the interest of its members.
  • Anyone can act in the public interest.

The High Court has jurisdiction to hear these claims and can grant a wide range of remedies, including declarations, orders, and compensation.

5. Practical Steps When Your Rights Are Violated

  • Document everything: Record dates, events, and the people involved.
  • Preserve evidence: Keep records, correspondence and witnesses' details.
  • Seek legal advice early: Constitutional petitions have specific procedures.
  • Act promptly: Delay can weaken your claim.
